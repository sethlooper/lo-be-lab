§ 03 / Research program

The lab is organized
around a small number
of open questions.

Each project contributes evidence, usually partial, toward one or more of them. The questions outlast any single tool. Below: the six animating questions, the projects that address each, and what I have learned so far. My framework paper, The Architecture of Becoming (Looper, 2026; Working Report 2026-06), articulates the four-part design vocabulary that organizes the research program. Methods and references live on separate pages.
Research interests

I study how spatial, narrative and computational environments, AI tools among them, help students reflect during career and life transitions, especially in design disciplines. The method is design-based research: build a program, run it with a cohort, watch what happens, revise. Each current thread has a working paper: spatial interfaces for career exploration, peer dialogue as a structure for reflection, career tools built for design students, the architectural portfolio as a place where narrative identity forms, and AI that reflects a student's thinking instead of writing for them. A newer direction looks at how design schools and firms work together to bring students into practice. The work draws on architectural pedagogy, narrative identity theory and research on how people read visual narratives.

Theoretical anchors: Schön (reflective practitioner), Shulman (signature pedagogies), McAdams (narrative identity), Cohn (visual narrative comprehension), Lave & Wenger (situated learning).

The program

Lo/Be Lab runs one long inquiry: how designed environments help people reflect their way through life transitions. The emerging theory is named The Architecture of Becoming. Like any design-based research program, the theory is transitional: it is worked out through the projects, not asserted in advance, and it is revised as the evidence comes in.

Animating questions
RQ 1
Can designed environments and guided conversation help people reflect during life transitions at the scale of a full undergraduate cohort, beyond a single session?

The question is whether reflection, historically a one-on-one, high-touch practice, can be made systemic without becoming hollow. The Dartmouth programs (2024-2026) placed my tools inside the rhythm of an academic term.

Explored in
DartWorld, Career Design Lab
Method
Design iteration across terms (2024-2026), documented through design review and instructor field notes. No quantitative or qualitative study of student data was conducted.
Documented in
DartWorld working paper (Working Report 2026-03)
RQ 2
What design principles help spatial, conversational, and computational tools support people in seeing, reframing, and making sense of their own stories?

The interface question. Most reflection tools are conceptually ambitious and graphically generic. I treat the surface, spatial map vs. ranked list, single word vs. detailed profile, paper card vs. drag-and-drop, as part of the cognition, not a wrapper around it.

Explored in
Synapse, DartWorld, Narrative by Design
Method
Design comparison of interface formats; instructor field notes. Behavioral observation and analysis of student artifacts are planned and have not been conducted.
Documented in
Synapse working paper (Working Report 2026-01)
RQ 3
Does personal reflection make people better at group decision-making? Can tools that help individuals understand themselves also improve how communities talk to each other?

My work started from individual reflection. An open question is whether the deliberative skills that grow from sustained personal reflection, listening, holding contradiction, suspending judgment, also surface in group contexts. Participatory design research is the early site for this.

Explored in
Career Design Lab (peer-dyad work; facilitated transitions)
Method
Design rationale for the compression-then-deliberation sequencing; comparison of conversation quality and qualitative analysis of recorded sessions are open empirical lines awaiting a consent and review process.
Documented in
Career Design Lab working paper (Working Report 2026-02)
RQ 4
How can AI surface evidence about a person without replacing their own judgment about what it means?

LLMs are good at saying confident things about people, and that is not the job here. The design question is how to use models as mirrors, surfacing the gap between what someone says about themselves and what their resume, writing, or card sorts show, without prescribing what to do with it.

Explored in
Narrative by Design (the AI-as-mirror layer)
Method
Design rationale for the mirror-vs-advisor distinction; A/B comparison of student response quality with and without AI prompts, and post-session interviews, are planned and would first need a consent process.
Documented in
Narrative by Design working paper (Working Report 2026-04)
RQ 5
What's specific to a discipline, and what's universal, when designing reflection tools for a particular profession?

General-audience tools work across populations. Field-specific tools go deeper but narrower. Threshold gave me a chance to ask: which parts of the work generalize, and which parts have to be re-built for each profession? The answer matters for whether my work can extend beyond a single institution.

Explored in
Threshold (architecture, landscape architecture, urban design)
Method
Comparison of discipline-specific tools against equivalent general-audience tools; informal consultation with CAED faculty and with students in my courses (formal interview research with students in the discipline has not been done).
Documented in
Threshold working paper (Working Report 2026-05)
RQ 6
Who decides which technologies mediate student development, on what knowledge, and what would it take to choose and design them well?

The institutional question behind the others. Campuses adopt ed-tech through logics of efficiency and signaling, often without the faculty and students who hold the relevant knowledge, and the costs land on students. I ask what a literacy-led, participatory alternative looks like, and whether a student-centered design can answer the critique without reproducing the datafication it opposes.

Explored in
Curriculus (concept work); my critical-review work
Method
Critical/integrative review of ed-tech governance paired with research through design; reflexive evaluation of my own design proposition against its own critique.
Documented in
From Buying Solutions to Designing Becoming working paper (Working Report 2026-09)
Design principles

The principles below are commitments that organize my design decisions. Each could be tested; none has been yet. The full design vocabulary (reflection, interpretation, visualization, action) is set out in my framework paper, The Architecture of Becoming.

Reflection before action
Students who spend time on their own patterns and priorities before jumping to job listings, alumni networks, or industry research make better decisions. This is a design premise, not a measured result.
Student data, primary
The best data about a student comes from the student: what they write, what they say, and what they notice about themselves through structured reflection, rather than what the institution tracks about them.
Interpretation without prescription
My tools show what might be there without saying what it means. Students work through what they see in conversation with peers and facilitators.
Making thinking visible
Career maps, pathway diagrams, and spatial layouts help students see connections they can't hold in their heads. When you can look at your own thinking from the outside, you notice things you'd otherwise miss.
Connected systems
I build tools that connect what students, advisors, and employers see.
Methodology

The methods catalog below describes the lab's intended evidence program. At the current stage, I have no IRB-reviewed protocol, and the project reports use no research data; deployments were documented through my instructor field notes, design review, and design-rationale write-ups. The empirical studies that would require consent and review infrastructure are documented as open research lines in each working paper. The one empirical study so far is the research assistant's working paper, Negotiating Selfhood with AI (Pan, 2026), which collected survey and interview data with participants' consent and was not reviewed by an institutional review board.

Design-based research

Every project starts as a prototype. I ran the Dartmouth programs (2024-2026) with students as founding manager of the Career Design Lab; Threshold is informed by my teaching at Kent State CAED. I reviewed each version as facilitator and lead designer, documented it in field notes, and used it to build the next version. Each tool is a design hypothesis, and each academic term was a design-iteration cycle.

Mixed methods (intended)

Quantitative (intended once consent infrastructure is in place): engagement traces from Synapse and DartWorld; pre/post measures of clarity and decision-confidence; behavioral observation of map vs. list interaction. Current documentation, which is not a study: my instructor field notes and design review. Computational text analysis of student-produced material is in scope under a future consent process.

Participatory design

Everything was iterated in working programs rather than simulated: Dartmouth College programs (2024-2026) and, for Threshold, my teaching at Kent State University's College of Architecture and Environmental Design (CAED). I design alongside the students I work with; formal human-subjects research with those students is planned and has not started.

Theoretical grounding
Foundations

The lab draws on five intersecting literatures: learning sciences (reflection, metacognition, distributed cognition), identity development theory (self-authorship, possible selves), narrative psychology (life stories, narrative identity), sensemaking research (Weick, Klein), and human-computer interaction (external representations, cognitive load, ambient interfaces). I treat career development as a form of learning, not a transaction, which means it needs environments that support sustained reflection over time.

Key works

Baxter Magolda (2001) on self-authorship; Bransford, Brown & Cocking (2000) on how people learn; Schön (1983) on reflective practice; Weick (1995) on sensemaking; Mezirow (1997) on transformative learning; Kolb (1984) on experiential learning; Markus & Nurius (1986) on possible selves; Norman (1993) and Kirsh (2010) on external cognitive supports; McAdams (1993) on the stories we live by.

On the design-management and firm-side traditions, the work also draws on Shulman (2005) on signature pedagogies, Person & Snelders (2010) and Person (2016, 2023) on design integration in firms, Junginger (2009, 2015) on design absorption, and Liedtka (2015) on design thinking in management.

Current working paper
Title

The Architecture of Becoming: A Four-Part Framework for Designing Career Development Systems as Longitudinal Learning Environments

The paper argues that most career platforms are built for the institution, not the student, prioritizing placement metrics and operational efficiency while ignoring the reflective work students need to do. It proposes a four-part framework: reflection (structured writing and exercises), interpretation (tools that surface patterns without prescribing meaning), visualization (maps and diagrams that externalize thinking), and action (job search, mentorship, skill-building, sequenced after self-knowledge).

Open threads & future directions
Longitudinal validation
Comparing student clarity and goal stability across tools over multiple terms. Do the gains from structured reflection last after the program ends?
Organizational transition
What does it take to move an institution from counting placements and job-board clicks to measuring whether students can tell their own story and make decisions they believe in?
AI and autonomy
Making sure AI tools support student thinking rather than replace it. The open question is how language models can help people analyze their own stories without overriding their judgment.
Generalizability
Building a second discipline-specific tool (beyond Threshold/architecture) to test which parts of the framework generalize across professions and which need to be rebuilt.

Theoretical lineage

My work sits within several intersecting research traditions. On the education side: Donald Schön's reflective practitioner (1983) and his analysis of the architecture studio as exemplar of professional education (1987); Lee Shulman's account of signature pedagogies (2005); situated learning and communities of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998). On the firm side: the empirical design management tradition associated with Oscar Person and the SDIM group at Aalto University, Sabine Junginger, Jeanne Liedtka, and Rachel Cooper & Mike Press. Methodologically, the work is anchored in design-based research (Brown, 1992; Barab & Squire, 2004) and reflective practice.
