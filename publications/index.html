Negotiating Selfhood with AI: Chinese International Students' Experiences of Cultural Values, Language, and Self-Reflection (2026). [pdf]
Pan, J. Working paper, November 2026. Jiaxi (Peggy) Pan, Boston University, research assistant at Lo/Be Lab.
Suggested citation: Pan, J. (2026). Negotiating Selfhood with AI: Chinese International Students' Experiences of Cultural Values, Language, and Self-Reflection [Working paper]. Available at jiaxi-pan.github.io/research-selfhood.html
Data: Bilingual survey (43 responses) and 10 semi-structured interviews (eight in Mandarin, two in English); reflexive thematic analysis
Keywords: international students, generative AI, self-reflection, cultural self-construal, relational credit
Abstract

Chinese international students in U.S. higher education increasingly use conversational AI, including Western tools such as ChatGPT and Chinese tools such as Doubao, to reflect on their goals and sense of self. Drawing on the looking-glass self, self-construal theory and the Conservation of Resources model, this exploratory study combines a survey of 43 international students, 37 of them Chinese, with ten semi-structured interviews. Thirty-two of the 37 Chinese respondents reported using AI this way, most often for career and academic decisions. Students valued AI as a confidant that asks nothing back, which the paper describes with the tentative concept of relational credit. Some perceived AI as putting the individual first or as overly affirming, though these perceptions varied across tools. Students kept authority over the result, choosing languages and tools for different problems and rewriting or rejecting what AI offered. AI-mediated reflection appears less as cultural absorption than as a negotiated practice.

Roles: Jiaxi Pan revised the survey and interview questions, ran the survey, recruited participants, conducted and transcribed the interviews, analyzed the data, and wrote the paper. Seth Looper supervised the study at Lo/Be Lab.
Ethics: Participation was voluntary and every participant gave consent before taking part. The survey did not ask for names, interviews were recorded with permission, and participants are identified by number. The study was exploratory and was not reviewed by an institutional review board.

From Buying Solutions to Designing Becoming: The Ed-Tech Leadership Gap in Higher Education and a Student-Centered Career-Development Alternative (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-09.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026i). From Buying Solutions to Designing Becoming: The Ed-Tech Leadership Gap in Higher Education and a Student-Centered Career-Development Alternative. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-09. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-09. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Critical EdTech, Institutional Design, Career Design, Reflexive Design-Based Research
Keywords: educational technology, ed-tech governance, technological solutionism, datafication, procurement, predictive analytics, automated proctoring, student-centered design, research through design, reflexive design, career development, pedagogy of care
Abstract

Higher-education institutions have become structurally dependent on commercial educational-technology platforms to mediate teaching, advising, retention, and career development. This paper argues that this dependence is governed by leaders, provosts, deans, CIOs, student-affairs and career-services executives, who adopt tools under logics of efficiency, risk-management, and institutional signaling, frequently without the technical, pedagogical, ethical, and evaluative literacy to assess what they procure, and without the faculty and students who hold the relevant knowledge. The result is an adoption-understanding gap: campuses accumulate fragmented, often surveillant systems while the problems they claim to solve, belonging, equity, learning, well-being, and meaningful career formation, persist or worsen, and students bear the cost. Synthesizing critical ed-tech studies, higher-education administration, and design scholarship, the paper establishes the critique with current evidence (market concentration; weak self-reported benefit despite near-universal adoption; documented equity harms in predictive analytics and automated proctoring; the recurring "local success, scaled null" pattern). It then asks a constructive design question: what would career development look like if it began from students' need to reflect, make sense, and become, rather than from institutional throughput? In response it presents Curriculus, a three-stakeholder (student, advisor, employer) concept centering student-generated reflective data ("dots and lines"), longitudinal career-pathway visualization, skill self-assessment, and human mentorship. The paper also evaluates Curriculus reflexively, showing where it answers the critique and where its own AI scoring, analytics, and employer-matching risk reproducing the datafication and bias it opposes, and proposes safeguards. The contribution is a paired diagnosis and design proposition, plus principles for literacy-led, student-centered technology governance.

The Architecture of Becoming: A Four-Part Framework for Designing Career Development Systems as Longitudinal Learning Environments (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-06.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026f). The Architecture of Becoming: A Four-Part Framework for Designing Career Development Systems as Longitudinal Learning Environments. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-06. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-06. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Career Design, Reflective Learning, Institutional Design, Design-Based Research
Keywords: career development, institutional design, longitudinal learning, reflection-first pedagogy, high-impact practices, self-authorship, narrative identity, planned happenstance, design-based research, framework paper
Abstract

Most institutional career platforms are built for the institution, not the student. They prioritize placement metrics and operational efficiency while underbuilding the slower reflective work the students themselves need to do. Students end up moving between disconnected tools (resume builders, skill inventories, advising systems, networking platforms) each of which shows a different version of them, with no way to connect the pieces over time. This paper proposes a four-part framework for redesigning career development systems as longitudinal learning environments: reflection (structured writing and exercises through which the student articulates what they know about themselves); interpretation (tools that surface patterns in the reflective material without prescribing what the patterns mean); visualization (maps and diagrams that externalize thinking so the student can see their own configurations); and action (job search, mentorship, and skill-building deployed after self-knowledge rather than before it). The framework is grounded in higher-education research on high-impact practices (Kuh, 2008; Pascarella & Terenzini, 2005), constructionist accounts of vocational behavior (Savickas, 2013; McAdams, 1993), and the design-based research tradition (Brown, 1992; Design-Based Research Collective, 2003). It informed the design of five working prototypes that Lo/Be Lab built and revised across academic terms (2024–2026), each documented in a companion working report. The paper argues that the dominant institutional pattern (career development as service delivery) should be replaced by a pattern that treats career development as scaffolded learning across the academic term.

A Place to Walk Through: Spatial Narrative Platforms for Sustained Career Reflection at Cohort Scale (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-03.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026c). A Place to Walk Through: Spatial Narrative Platforms for Sustained Career Reflection at Cohort Scale. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-03. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-03. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Reflective Learning, Spatial Computing, Identity Development, Design-Based Research
Keywords: career development, identity formation, longitudinal reflection, immersive learning environments, design-based research, 3D educational platforms, narrative identity, self-authorship, expressive writing, archetype clustering
Abstract

Career counseling at most universities operates on a high-touch, low-frequency model: one student, one advisor, one thirty-minute session. As a delivery model it is humane. As a developmental support, it is structurally mismatched to the work it is asked to do, because career formation in undergraduates is a longitudinal identity process (Baxter Magolda, 2001; Marcia, 1966) rather than a one-time decision amenable to advice. This paper describes DartWorld, a spatial narrative platform built at Dartmouth College with student developer teams from Dartmouth's DALI Lab (2024–2026) to explore whether a sustained, environment-shaped reflection practice can scale to a full undergraduate cohort. The platform integrates three components: a reflective onboarding that offers one of three archetypes (Explorer, Seeker, Achiever) as a provisional starting frame (the archetypes are emergent design categories, not boxes students are sorted into); a navigable three-dimensional environment for treating career exploration as a place to walk through rather than a list to scroll; and a journaling pinboard that persists across sessions and supports longitudinal review. Development followed a design-based research methodology (Brown, 1992; Design-Based Research Collective, 2003) with rolling ten-week iterations alongside successive student development teams. I argue, drawing on Schön's reflective-practitioner tradition (Schön, 1983), Kolb's experiential learning cycle (Kolb, 1984), and Dede's immersive-environments research (Dede, 2009), that DartWorld is most properly understood not as a digital career service but as a sustained reflective environment.

From Ranked Match to Spatial Map: A Design-Based Inquiry into Career Exploration Interface Affordances (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-01.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026a). From Ranked Match to Spatial Map: A Design-Based Inquiry into Career Exploration Interface Affordances. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-01. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-01. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Career Design, Spatial Computing, Design-Based Research, Vocational Psychology
Keywords: career assessment, vocational psychology, design-based research, information visualization, choice overload, position bias, spatial cognition, RIASEC, narrative identity, design rationale
Abstract

The standard output of computer-driven career assessment is a ranked list of occupations sorted by match score. This paper argues that the ranked-list format imports two well-documented interface failures into the assessment context: position bias, which structurally undervalues options past the first three results (Joachims et al., 2005; Pan et al., 2007), and choice overload, which produces decision paralysis when the option set becomes too large (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Chernev et al., 2015). Together these failures foreclose the very exploratory work that career assessment is meant to enable. I describe Synapse, a working prototype that replaces the ranked-list output with a spatial map of 460 occupations drawn from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and the O*NET system. Students answer thirteen forced-choice questions; results are presented as a position within a continuous occupational landscape, with semantically similar careers placed in spatial proximity. I position the work within design-based research (Brown, 1992; Design-Based Research Collective, 2003) and contemporary constructionist accounts of vocational behavior (Savickas, 2002, 2013). The contribution is a design rationale and a deployable artifact, not a controlled effectiveness evaluation; I close with a research agenda for the empirical work the design move requires. The paper is part of an ongoing research program at Lo/Be Lab on environments that support reflective work during life transitions.

The Deliberation Cohort: Six Tools, One Arc. A Term-Long Program for Identity Sensemaking at Cohort Scale (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-02.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026b). The Deliberation Cohort: Six Tools, One Arc. A Term-Long Program for Identity Sensemaking at Cohort Scale. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-02. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-02. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Career Design, Identity Development, Reflective Learning, Design-Based Research, Deliberation Theory
Keywords: deliberation, identity sensemaking, cohort learning, self-authorship, zone of proximal development, signature pedagogies, narrative identity, design-based research, peer reflection, career development programs
Abstract

Most university career programs are organized around discrete services (advising sessions, resume reviews, job-board access). They rarely scaffold the slower, longitudinal work of identity development that precedes informed career choice. This paper documents the Career Design Lab (CDL), a term-long program at Dartmouth College (2024–2026) that integrated six tools (Identity Mapping Studio, Tile Sorting, Pattern Distillation, OneWord, Launch Studio, and a recursive Sequence framework) into a single deliberation arc taken by cohorts of 15 to 30 students. Each tool was developed and tested independently before being integrated into the program; each academic term was both a delivered program and a design-iteration cycle that informed the next version. I argue, drawing on deliberation theory (Fishkin, 2009; Yankelovich, 1991), on the developmental-psychology literature on self-authorship (Baxter Magolda, 2001), on Vygotsky's account of the zone of proximal development (Vygotsky, 1978), and on Shulman's framework of signature pedagogies (Shulman, 2005), that the program's defining design move is to treat career formation as a signature pedagogy of undergraduate education rather than as a service.

Compression with a Witness: Narrative by Design, AI-as-Mirror, and the 90-Minute Workshop Format (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-04.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026d). Compression with a Witness: Narrative by Design, AI-as-Mirror, and the 90-Minute Workshop Format. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-04. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-04. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Career Design, AI-Mediated Reflection, Narrative Identity, Identity Development
Keywords: narrative identity, compression, card-sort triangulation, AI as mirror, expressive writing, disorienting dilemma, facilitated workshop, time-constrained pedagogy, self-authorship, generative AI in education
Abstract

Career advising at the institutional level is dominated by the resume and the interview script. Both formats presume that students already know what they want to say about themselves and need help saying it more effectively. Many students do not lack experience; they lack a structured method for finding the meaningful pattern inside what they have already done. This paper documents Narrative by Design (NBD), a 90-minute facilitated workshop developed at Dartmouth College that supports narrative discovery through three operations: a compression sequence that takes the student from a free-form paragraph to a single defended word; three card sorts adapted from established instruments (Knowdell Career Values, a strengths card sort informed by CliftonStrengths themes, and Knowdell Motivated Skills) that triangulate the student's values, strengths, and skills; and an AI-assisted mirror layer that surfaces gaps between the student's self-description and the evidence the card sorts produce. I draw on McAdams's narrative identity theory (McAdams, 1993, 2001), Pennebaker's research on expressive writing (Pennebaker, 1997), Mezirow's transformative learning framework (Mezirow, 1997), and recent work on generative AI in education (Mollick & Mollick, 2023). I argue that the 90-minute time constraint is itself a pedagogical design move and that the AI layer functions as mirror, not advisor.

Crit, Career, and Discipline: Threshold and the Test Case for Discipline-Specific Reflection Tooling (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-05.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026e). Crit, Career, and Discipline: Threshold and the Test Case for Discipline-Specific Reflection Tooling. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-05. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-05. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Career Design, Architecture Education, Discipline-Specific Tooling, Design-Based Research
Keywords: architecture education, signature pedagogies, design studio, career construction theory, discipline-specific career tooling, studio crit, professional identity formation, career adaptability, reflective practitioner, critique-as-reflection
Abstract

Generic career resources rarely speak to the field-specific economic and cultural realities of architecture, where licensure pathways, portfolio culture, firm hierarchies, and the studio-crit pedagogy shape decisions that other career tools cannot anticipate. This paper documents Threshold, a discipline-specific career toolkit for architecture, landscape architecture, and urban design students and emerging professionals. The platform combines a verified searchable database of architecture firms across the United States with reflection exercises adapted to the studio critique format that students already practice in their formal training. I draw on Lee Shulman's framework of signature pedagogies (Shulman, 2005), Donald Schön's analyses of the architectural studio (Schön, 1985, 1987), the empirical and critical literature on the design crit as ritual (Anthony, 1991; Webster, 2007), and Career Construction Theory's account of career adaptability (Savickas, 2013; Savickas & Porfeli, 2012). I argue that reflection methods generalize across professions but reflection content does not, and that respecting the existing signature pedagogy of a discipline is a prerequisite for any tool seeking adoption within it.

Anchor and Hold Space: Inclusive Leadership as Narrative Co-Authorship in a Two-Hour Workshop for International Undergraduate Peer Mentors (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-07.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026g). Anchor and Hold Space: Inclusive Leadership as Narrative Co-Authorship in a Two-Hour Workshop for International Undergraduate Peer Mentors. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-07. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-07. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Inclusive Leadership, Mentor Training, International Student Affairs, ESL-Conscious Workshop Design, Design-Based Research
Keywords: inclusive leadership, narrative co-authorship, international undergraduate peer mentoring, ESL-conscious workshop design, Life Design pedagogy, Knowdell Career Values, narrative compression, responsive workshop design, design-based research, workshop pedagogy
Abstract

This paper documents the design rationale of a two-hour mentor-training workshop delivered to international undergraduate peer mentors at Dartmouth College in May 2026, in which inclusive leadership is reframed as narrative co-authorship: the practice of staying anchored in one's own narrative so that one can hold space for someone else to find theirs. I position this framing as a relational stance rather than the competency cluster that dominates the inclusive leadership literature (Bourke & Espedido, 2019; Hewlett, Marshall, & Sherbin, 2013), and translate the stance into a workshop architecture that adapts Stanford Life Design (Burnett & Evans, 2016) for a non-native-English-speaking undergraduate mentor population. The workshop's pedagogical signature is a designed sequence combining narrative compression, the Knowdell Career Values Card Sort, and a compressed Odyssey Plan, intended to produce a values-tested anchor word each mentor can carry into the fall match-introductions. An informal pre-workshop check-in, run by the program, shaped which modules got emphasis; its responses are not reported here. I document a set of ESL-conscious design practices developed across an extended iteration cycle, name what was cut for time and why, and offer the workshop architecture as a replicable design vocabulary for practitioners building leadership development for similar audiences. The workshop was delivered once and no post-workshop evaluation was conducted; this paper is a design rationale, not an outcomes report.

Portfolio as Narrative: Reframing Architectural Identity Construction (2026). [pdf]
Looper, S. Lo/Be Lab Working Reports, 2026-08.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026h). Portfolio as Narrative: Reframing Architectural Identity Construction. Lo/Be Lab Working Report 2026-08. New Haven, CT. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/2026-08. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Architectural Pedagogy, Portfolio, Design Education, Narrative Identity, Conceptual Framework
Keywords: architectural pedagogy, portfolio, narrative identity, visual narrative comprehension, reflective practice, signature pedagogies, boundary object, design education
Abstract

The architectural portfolio is taught, almost everywhere, as a graphic compilation problem. Yet the work it is asked to do is identity work: helping a student see, and then author, the through-line that turns a folder of disparate projects into the trajectory of a single designer. That work is formative throughout a design education, and it becomes visible wherever the portfolio is read closely, including at the school-to-practice transition. This paper argues that the architectural portfolio is best understood not as a graphic-compilation problem but as a genre-and-identity problem, and that reframing it accordingly transforms what the portfolio studio can do. The paper presents a pedagogical framework developed across iterations of Architectural Narratives, a hybrid graduate and undergraduate portfolio studio at Kent State University's College of Architecture and Environmental Design. The framework integrates two theoretical anchors that the design-education literature has tended to read separately: Cohn's framework of visual narrative comprehension and McAdams's narrative identity theory. It puts them to work through four pedagogical moves: reframing the genre, discovering the red thread, structuring the narrative arc, and translating cognitive load into layout. As a conceptual paper, it does not present empirical evaluation of student outcomes; it offers the framework as a structured pedagogical proposition, grounded in studio practice and theoretical synthesis. The reframing has implications for architectural curricula, for portfolio assessment, and for wider conversations in design pedagogy about authentic learning, designerly well-being (Stables, 2013), and signature pedagogies (Shulman, 2005).

Steering vs. Becoming: A Map of Reflection Tools for Life Transitions (2026). [read]
Looper, S. Working synthesis, state of the field.
Suggested citation: Looper, S. (2026). Steering vs. Becoming: A Map of Reflection Tools for Life Transitions (Working synthesis). Lo/Be Lab. Available at sethlooper.github.io/lo-be-lab/publications/steering-vs-becoming. ORCID: 0009-0002-8683-1632
Categories: Decision Science, Behavioral Design, Reflective Learning, Theory
Keywords: nudge, boost, choice architecture, self-determination theory, autonomy-preserving design, life transitions, design-based research
Abstract

Decision science has been successful at steering behavior at scale, but it has rarely been turned on the high-stakes, identity-laden transitions that matter most to the people living them. This synthesis separates two intents that the literature usually blurs: steering (the nudge), which engineers the environment toward a designer-chosen outcome, and becoming (the boost), which builds the chooser's own competence so the benefit persists. Mapping reflection tools on two axes, steering to becoming and single-session to longitudinal, locates an under-occupied quadrant, becoming and longitudinal, and argues that life transitions are its natural home. It places Lo/Be Lab's projects in that quadrant and connects the argument to The Architecture of Becoming.
